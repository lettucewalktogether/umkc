# Plain language

Target: an eighth-grade reader understands it the first time. Based on the US
Federal Plain Language Guidelines (plainlanguage.gov).

This applies to everything a person reads. Site copy, chat replies, emails,
handouts, comments, commit messages. There is no audience that benefits from
being made to work harder.

## The rules

**Lead with what the reader needs.** Put the answer first, the reasoning after.
Never make someone read three sentences of setup to reach the point.

**One idea per sentence.** Aim under 20 words. If a sentence has two commas and
an "and", it is probably two sentences.

**Active voice, named actor.** "Marsha reviews the scores", not "the scores are
reviewed". Passive voice hides who does what.

**Everyday words.** Use the shortest word that is still accurate:

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| utilize, leverage | use |
| prior to, in advance of | before |
| in order to | to |
| commence, initiate | start |
| terminate | end, stop |
| additional | more |
| sufficient | enough |
| demonstrate | show |
| facilitate | help |
| regarding, pertaining to | about |
| in the event that | if |
| at this point in time | now |

**Technical terms: plain word first, term in brackets.** The term still has to
be learned, so keep it - but lead with the meaning:

- "money set aside for a purchase (an encumbrance)"
- "permission to spend (an appropriation)"
- "money the City owes but has not paid (a payable)"

Do this on first use on each page. After that the term alone is fine.

**Say what a number means.** A number without a reading is work handed to the
reader. "4.8 out of 7" says nothing; "confident - can explain and apply it in
familiar situations (4.8 out of 7)" says something. Every statistic, score, and
percentage gets a sentence a non-specialist can act on.

**Name the thing, not the mechanism.** Headings should say what the reader is
looking at: "Students who completed the assessment", not "Completion". "How
sure students sound", not "Epistemic stance".

**Cut what does not earn its place.** Every sentence that explains the method
rather than the finding, restates the heading, or hedges an already-hedged
claim is noise. Padding is not politeness - it buries the useful part.

## Words to avoid outright

Unless the sentence breaks without them: epistemic, normative, heuristic,
paradigm, operationalise, methodology (say "method"), utilise, aforementioned,
henceforth, thereof, pursuant to, notwithstanding.

Never use "simply", "just", "obviously", or "of course" about a task the reader
may find hard.

## Checking the work

Before shipping any text:

1. Read the first sentence alone. Does it carry the point?
2. Any sentence over 25 words - split it.
3. Any word in the table above - replace it.
4. Any technical term - is it explained on first use?
5. Any number - does a sentence say what it means?
6. Read it aloud. Anywhere you stumble, the reader stumbles harder.

## What this does not mean

Plain language is not dumbing down. It does not mean dropping accuracy,
removing necessary caveats, or pretending something is simpler than it is.

When something is genuinely uncertain, say so plainly. "We cannot tell who did
not submit. A student who did nothing leaves no record." That beats both jargon
and false confidence.
